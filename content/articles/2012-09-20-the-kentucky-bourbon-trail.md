# The Kentucky Bourbon Trail

_(missing image)_ (**caption:** Delish; **alt:** Heaven Hill Warehouse (there's over a billion dollars of booze here))

I’ve always enjoyed bourbon in the sense that drinking it makes you feel like a down home country gentleman, even if you’re mixing it with coke just to get (sour) mashed (which means a small portion of the previous fermentation is added to a new batch to speed the process). There’s even a reason for this statesman-like quality as bourbon was marketed as the drink of the modern aristocrat once American beer consumption overtook that of spirits in the 1800′s. However, now that I’ve been through half of the bourbon trail in Kentucky where 95% of the world’s supply is distilled, I might just start enjoying a glass on its own from time to time.

You see, bourbon is no different than Scotch or Irish whiskey, it’s just the ratio of the grains that are fermented, what type of barrel they are aged in, and for how long. In fact, most of the original crafters of bourbon were all of Scottish or Irish decent, corn just happens to be in abundance in America, and there still is rye, wheat or barley mixed in depending on which brand you’re tasting. Some of the used bourbon barrels are even sent to the UK these days for re-use in whiskey aging because due to one of those funny jurisdictional classification laws like Champagne or Agave Tequila, Kentucky Bourbon can only be crafted from a barrel made of freshly charred, virgin oak.

One of those “funny laws” that really makes a difference as it pertains to Kentucky is the water here. Central Kentucky, where all the distilleries are located within 100 miles of eachother, lies on a bed of limestone that naturally filters the water causing it to have a high calcium, low iron content and generally be free of contaminants, which is what gives bourbon it’s clean, smooth taste. Clean and smooth is what I have been conditioned to say after all the tourism videos at the distillery tours, it’s still goddam whiskey for Christ’s sake.

However, it could be worse. I learned the term “gut rot” originated from a type of whiskey/moonshine that used to have lead additivites that made it taste so smooth and sweet you would only want that type of whiskey, until you died because your stomach lining and intestinal tract were dead from lead poisoning. Or even better, to seal up whiskey barrels, naturally there’s a small hole, into which a small stopper is hammered into place, the stopper being called the bung. And so where does the stopper get stuck into? The bung hole! Beavis and Butthead must have been bourbon fans.

Anyways, history aside, I always used to drink Wild Turkey because it’s prevalent, cheap, and has a turkey on the bottle, but I don’t believe I ever will again (OK, that’s a lie for effect, blow me). Not only was their tour the most homogenized vanilla crap of any due to their ownership by a giant multinational liquor conglormerate, the stuff also tasted like swill…and when I say vanilla crap, I mean it, there’s actually vanillan extract inside oak wood.

Naturally all the tours end with a fine tasting of the better than average stuff they want you to buy, but the Wild Turkey Rare Breed and all the other bullshit they were hocking were awful. To make matters worse, on account of their multinational ownership, they were the only distillery I visited that sends all their alcohol to somewhere random in Arkansas to be bottled, not on site.

I could go on at great length about how the suspended solids in bourbon adhere to ice, why it should be put in the refridgerator, not room temperature or the freezer prior to serving and how the location of the barrel inside the….oh, OK, that one’s really cool. You know how a good Scotch will be something like a 23 year old, whereas a good bourbon can be found in the 10 year range? The reason is the temperature in Kentucky. Because it gets so much hotter here in the summer, more of the liquid in the barrel will be forced into the wood, only to be sucked back out in the sub-zero winter temperatures, thereby absorbing more flavour in less time. That’s also why which storey on the 7, sometimes 8 tall warehouses the barrel is stored will affect whether it winds up a in a blended or small batch final product, versus the eponymous “single barrel”, which are the ones aged in what they call the “honey spots” because it gets so much hotter on the top floors.

And, because wood is pourous and water molecules are smaller than alcohol, a batch that starts at 120 proof could easily end up at 130 proof. The entire warehouse smells distinctly of bourbon because of this leakage, which is roughly 3% a year and called the “angel’s share”, so some of the very expensive craft bourbons aged over 20 years wind up being removed with a barrel that’s half full. And on of the main reason’s they’re so expensive is because bourbon sitting on the shelf aging is taxed every year regardless to the tune of half of the state of Kentucky’s revenue!

Uhhh, I need to stop. The answer to the ultimate question is Eagle Rare Single Barrel, trust me. It’s $27 in Kentucky and $60 in Canada, so I just need to convert Bessie into a bootlegging machine and I’m good to go.
