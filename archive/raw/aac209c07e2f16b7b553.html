I’m Still Here

Blimpy On January - 11 - 2011

Other than the fact I’m not a limitlessly talented actor and don’t have millions in my bank account, I’m pretty sure the biggest lacking parallel between me and Joaquin Phoenix is that I can’t grow such a natty beard.

I am of course referring to the recent flash in the pan popular documentary about Phoenix’s supposed collapse from Johnny Cash and Gladiator Oscar nominated actor to coke snorting, hooker banging reject-all reject.  I just got the chance to finally watch I’m Still Here last week after incessantly talking it up my entire trip, and while one of the best descriptors I can think of for the movie itself is jarring, I was nonetheless thoroughly impressed.

For those of you not familiar with the story, Joaquin Phoenix decided after his 2008 movie Two Lovers with Gwyneth Paltrow that he was done with acting, wanted to pursue a career in hip hop and generally quit giving a fuck about everything, especially the Hollywood marketing machine.  Problem is, it was all an act…except it wasn’t, which is the entire brilliance of it.  And I use the term brilliance reluctantly, it was completely self-indulgent, disrespectful to a lot of people and borderline insane.  Granted, that’s exactly how I like it, but as some of my Hollywood friends pointed out, that first disaster interview he gave on Letterman may very well have cost some people their jobs.  But you can’t make an omelette without breaking a few eggs.

Letterman actually played it as well as he could have, while still remaining shallow and condescending, which are his true colours.  Joaquin’s shone through as well because when I say this was an act but not an act, whatever it was totally sabotaged his career costing him millions in the process and took more balls than any Hollywood actor you may perhaps ever see.  It’s one thing to self destruct, hell the same could be said about fattening cue card reading Marlon Brando, but at least he still played ball.  Joaquin played ball only to prove that it was all a ball game.  And by turning it into such a grandiose absurd spectacle, all the lines start to blur as the “mock” intentions led to easily foreseeable real consequences.

He and director Casey Affleck would go on to call this a commentary on the nature of media and celebrity, but to think the extrapolations end there would be foolish, and I’m living proof.  Countless times in his supposed revelationary introspective moments Phoenix repeated the following two mantras.  One, that doing what other people say, standing where they tell you and saying what they wrote is not self-expression, it’s imprisonment.  Two, that we are all embroiled in an act of trying to be perceived by others.  I used to be guilty of the first, I am still guilty of the second.

Just watch the movie.  It’s hard going, really offensive and worth it.
