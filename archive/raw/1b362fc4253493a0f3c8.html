Almost From Scratch Tomato Sauce

Blimpy On August - 30 - 2010

This is the first recipe I ever learned how to properly gourmet cook and have been refining it for years.  Not only is it more delicious and hearty than any store-bought tomato sauce, the leftovers are reusable in a variety of other forms (chicken or eggplant parmesan, pizza) and it’s vegan-friendly.

Serves 8+ if you’re using it to serve with pasta

Ingredients

  • 1 large can crushed tomatoes
  • 1 small can tomato paste
  • ½ cup red wine (chose an acidic Italian wine like Chianti or Sangiovese to drink with the meal to compliment the acidity of the tomatoes)
  • 1 tsp balsamic vinegar
  • ½ a yellow onion, finely diced
  • ½ a carrot, finely diced
  • ½ a stick of celery, finely diced

    Eat fresh

  • 4 cloves garlic, finely diced
  • 3 white mushrooms, finely diced
  • 1 sweet red pepper, roasted
  • 1 shallot, roasted
  • 8 Campari or Roma tomatoes halved and deseeded, roasted
  • 2 tbsp olive oil
  • 1 tsp dried chilli pepper flakes
  • 1 tsp dried oregano
  • ½ tsp dried parsley
  • 2 tsp sea salt flakes
  • 1 tsp crushed black pepper
  • 10 whole white peppercorns
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 1 dried porcini mushroom (optional)
  • 1 sundried tomato (optional)
  • 3-4 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 6-8 leaves fresh basil, finely chopped
  • Fresh Italian parsley to garnish
  • Parmesan or Romano cheese

Preparation

Gathering all the above ingredients from the grocery store should be a relatively simple task, but if you can find a farmer’s market or fruit stand definitely opt for their higher quality, often cheaper produce.  The flavour of this dish stands alone solely on its raw ingredients, so you want them as high quality as possible, which I achieved for about $10 to $15.  Whatever you do, don’t substitute dried spices where fresh is called for, they will ruin the dish…well, from a purist perspective, but just trust me.

Roasting adds flavour and sweetness

As you can see, I like laying all the ingredients out at once, semi-organized in the order they’ll be cooked to make life easier.  This meal has multiple stages and requires attention for at least a couple hours, but isn’t a lot of work.  You’ll want to start creating the base of the sauce by dicing the onion, carrot, celery, garlic and white mushroom.  In a large sauce pot, add a tablespoon of olive oil with the onion, carrot and celery and turn the heat on low.  This varies from stove to stove, but you really don’t want to go above 2/10 on the heat dial.  This sauce base is referred to as mirepoix and should take 20-30 minutes to slowly break down and release its flavour.  Those first three ingredients are denser and take longer to cook, so after about 10 minutes you’ll want to stir in the garlic and mushrooms, then keep stirring and watching until the onions are translucent and the odour is sweet and rich, no longer bitter.  It’s a common misconception you’re supposed to brown the garlic and onions at the start of any sauce dish, not true at all, slow low heat is the correct and only way.

Over 2 hours of cooked-down, thick goodness

At this point, deglaze the pot by adding a quarter cup of the red wine and the balsamic vinegar and stirring briefly while working in the tomato paste.  A little bit of the salt and pepper can be used here, I generally add that in stages after tasting all the way along the cooking process.  The purpose of this small intricate step is to allow the mirepoix to fuse with the tomato flavour before it is thinned out and cooked down.  The thin acidic liquids will also strip all the remnants off the bottom of the pan and help break down and sweeten/enrich the whole sauce as cooking continues.

After letting the paste warm for 5 minutes, add the full can of crushed tomatoes, the chilli flakes, oregano, parsley, white peppercorns, bay leaf, dried porcini, sundried tomato, fresh thyme and remaining quarter cup of red wine.  Also add a cup of water and turn the heat up to around 3/10, whatever temperature will get it to a light simmer with a very slight amount of bubbling in the sauce.

Cooking

Now that the sauce is on its way, you’ve got about 2 hours to play with.  The longer you can cook down the tomatoes the richer it will taste, but with time as a factor you can cut cooking down to an hour and add less water if you like, it will just be more tomatoey and bitter.  Throughout the entire cook time you’ll need to stir about every 10 minutes making sure to get any sauce that’s stuck to the sides back in the soup.  Stirring also reduces bubbling and splashing by keeping the entire mixture an even temperature.  I sometimes cover the pot for the first 45 minutes just to allow it to hold temperature and get the initial cook, but it’s not required.  You’ll need to take the lid off at some point for the water to evaporate and it to cook down to a sauce thickness.  A handy accessory is one of those wire mesh splatter screens so you don’t wind up with tiny red dots all around your stove.

Everybody loves serving themselves

Using the spare time as the sauce cooks is where I add my own roasted vegetable flair that gives it a ton of flavour.  This time around I grabbed something like a big, sweet, red Anaheim pepper which gave it a bit more kick, but a red bell pepper works just as well.  Cut the top and bottom off the shallot and take off the skin, cut the fresh tomatoes in half and take the core and seeds out with a spoon and put them all in a bowl with the peppers.  I also only added 3 garlic cloves to the mirepoix and saved one to be roasted here.  In the large bowl, drizzle a tablespoon of olive oil over top, add a couple sprigs of finely chopped fresh thyme and salt and pepper and thoroughly mix by hand smearing the oil on all the surfaces.  Preheat a BBQ to medium high and lay all the vegetables on the roasting rack, turning every 5-10 minutes until done after about 25.

With the roasted red pepper, the skin should char up nicely so it can be peeled off, then cut lengthwise so the seeds and stem can be removed.  If you’re lucky the tomato skins can be removed too, but not required.  Throw all the roasted vegetables into a blender and churn them around a bit.  It doesn’t have to be a full blown paste or liquid, you just don’t want any big chunks.  You’ll want to add all the contents of the blender in with the cooking sauce when there is 30 to 45 minutes of cook time left.  I used to make the mistake of adding them right from the get-go, but continuing to cook for the full 2 hours completely mellows out the roasted flavour to the point you won’t notice it any more.  One other benefit to roasting the vegetables is they caramelize, bringing out their natural sweetness, which makes the usual teaspoon of sugar in a tomato sauce not necessary.

Especially when they wind up with this

Once the sauce is thickened up and tastes rich as hell (tasting along the way is key, especially for salt and pepper rationing) and you feel it’s “done”, you can get a pot of boiling water going for the pasta of your choice (I always use spaghetti, like I said, I’m a purist).  At this point, turn the heat off on the sauce, take out the bay leaf, thyme and sundried tomato and add the fresh basil, stirring thoroughly.  Again, if you add the basil too early it will cook too much and become bitter and you’ll lose that fresh herb bite.

Once the pasta is done cooking, strain it in a colander, put it in a serving bowl and stir in a couple spoonfuls of sauce so the pasta doesn’t stick to itself.  To serve I often have guests do it themselves depending how saucy they like it, but the idea is to spoon the pasta into a bowl, add a bunch of sauce over top, then some freshly grated parmesan and Italian parsley, stir together and enjoy.  A nice fluffy loaf of white bread is also amazing to sop up the plate remnants, or just scoop sauce right out of the pot, which I’ve also been known to do with a spoon or by the handful because it’s so good.  And good for you!
