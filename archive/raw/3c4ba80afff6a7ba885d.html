Album of the Week: Dwight Spitz by Count Bass D

Blimpy On August - 19 - 2010

This is real hip hop.  In my mind, one of the top 5 underground albums of the 2000’s.

Based in Nashville Tennessee, Count Bass D (real name Dwight Farrell) rose from the ashes of the late 90’s commercial versus underground battle, falling decidedly under the latter.  Championing innovation while remaining tied to the music’s roots and not concerned with record sales, I was almost shocked that I’ve been listening to this album for a solid 8 years and still enjoy it as much as the first time I heard it.

Dwight Spitz is a record full to the brim with ideas.  Functioning as both producer and rapper, Count Bass D uses his full creative palette to sculpt songs from nothing and definitely makes the most of it.  With 25 tracks, most clocking in at under 2 minutes, it continually stays fresh and interesting.  Rather than trying to lay down a sustained groove and throw a chorus over it to stretch things out to 4 minutes for radio play, Count Bass opts for all hooks.  Oh here’s a great idea for a minute and 30 seconds…oh, nope what about this fat beat…hold on, let’s try some jazz piano…wait, maybe work in a Doors sample…and so on.

Even more remarkable is out of this supreme cut-and-paste aesthetic the album still maintains a very consistent atmosphere.  Part of that can actually be attributed to the potpourri approach itself, but a lot has to do with the masterful use of samples employed.  Putting a 10 second speech snippet at the start or end of every couple tracks adds just as much to the message as what Count Bass has to rhyme about.  And I will definitely say his production skills are superior to his rapping, but the combination of the sometimes smooth, sometimes booming beats with his slightly nasal, laid back flow play off each other perfectly.

One of the best parts of the idea-a-minute approach of this album is that it yields some incredibly standout tracks (Sanctuary, August 25 2001, Make A Buck), but still something for everyone appeasing to varied tastes between listeners, and all the while not a single throw away track in the lot.  Sanctuary, using the aforementioned Doors sample, is probably my #1 ‘you have to listen to this’ joint of all time.  “The tags on my shoes cost more than your clothes, I’m out lookin for beats while y’all lookin for hoes” is brilliant posturing in the name of music over image.  Followed up in the same verse with “I’m a studio musician, not a studio gangsta” it gives a pretty good indication of what Dwight is all about.

Ultimately I don’t think either the underground or the bling factions in rap wound up winning the battle as they lost the war to the sweeping changes in the music industry, but if you want to hearken back to a simpler time when two turntables, a drum machine and a microphone could easily outdo all the coke and hookers Puff Daddy could summon, this album is an absolute must.
