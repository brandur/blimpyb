Blimpy On October - 16 - 2010

Deciding to abandon Bandon, I set off for the much sexier sounding Gold Beach.

Another boondocking gem

Seeing a couple of recommended roadside turnouts to camp at in my pimped out GPS, and having slept in one before no problem, I decided to go for it.  There were plenty along this winding, cliff-top stretch of Highway 101, mostly for viewpoints that I’m sure would have worked fine, but I figured these two spots were listed for a reason.  They were, as you can see from the attached shot taken the second I put the RV in park and jumped out.  Now with giant boulders draped in green, the scene had taken on a more rugged, Irish feeling, although I didn’t notice the green until the next day with it being sunset.

I should use my tripod more

Taking my usual waves and sunset shots, but this time with some nice big ol’ rocks for variety, I headed back to Bessie to call it a night…except it was 7 PM.  Once the darkness set in and I overcame the jitters I got when someone pulled into the turnout (likely to check their map, not to eat me) I noticed peering out my windows that I could see outside pretty darn well.  Perfectly clear skies and a big three quarter moon lit things up nicely enough that I wanted to bust out the tripod and try some night shots for the first time.

Just so you know it's dark out

Having never done this before, I decided to start big and jack the shutter speed waaay up to 13 seconds.  Woah!  Looking at the camera’s LCD screen startled me.  If I didn’t say anything, you’d think it was broad daylight, but no, the light’s all still there, our eyes just aren’t sensitive enough to see it in colours.  That freaked me out a lot, and it wasn’t until the next day I noticed another long shutter excursion somehow got me a shot of Mars, Venus or a satellite.  I don’t know which, but it sure wasn’t red like that when I was snapping pictures.

Return to light

I feel bad for all the people that pulled in at sunset or the following morning to take pictures for 2 minutes, as I spent a full 18 hours there.  The totality of the experience is something I absorbed to be a part of me, the pictures convey mere fragments of it.  Watching a full cycle of day to night and back, the stoic and jagged rocks only betrayed two hints of their transience, the rise and fall of the tide and how they reflected ambient light.  But wandering along the beach in bare feet, the sand here is very coarse, made of battered barnacle bits, and of course those very rocks.
