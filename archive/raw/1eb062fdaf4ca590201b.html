The Solar Series: System Sizing

Blimpy On August - 24 - 2010

Now that we’ve got an overall picture of what we’re trying to achieve in building an off-grid solar system (Goals, Rationale) and have some background on electricity (Power – AC & DC) it’s time to do a bit of thinking and a bit of math to turn that into reality.  This happens in two parts.  One, we need to understand what we’re trying to power and how much energy that will use.  Once we know that, the second task is determining what amount of solar panels and batteries will satisfy our needs once the system is built.

Step #1: Determine Energy Usage

For an RV system like mine, I’m just powering a subset of regular household items and appliances.  This includes a stereo, a laptop, lights, a coffee grinder and a water pump (naturally in order of importance, I’d rather have Led Zeppelin or hot caffeine than be able to wash dishes).  The first step is to make a list of everything you anticipate running, as you can see from my attached worksheet.

Doing the math to find 140 Amp hours total daily usage

The next step actually involves multiplication, just so you’re forewarned.  Knowing what you’re running is good, knowing the exact amount of electricity that takes is what you really need.  There are numerous resources online at a general level, such as this Wattage Chart.

Make a spreadsheet just like mine listing each component, its wattage and the number of hours a day it will need to run.  Multiplying wattage times hours for each gives a daily watt hour total, then adding all components together gives the total daily watt hour number.  This is all we are interested in, total electrical consumption as one number, once you reach this point each individual component being powered doesn’t matter anymore for calculation purposes, but you will probably go back and tweak that list a few times.

If you have any obscure devices you are looking to run that aren’t on a general online list, or if you are interested in knowing exactly how much energy an appliance uses, I highly recommend picking up something called a Kill-A-Watt.  These were available for free from the public library in Calgary so that might be an option in your town, or you can pick one up for $20.  Either way, it’s just a watt meter, you plug it in the wall in between your appliance, it has a little LCD display and can tell you exactly how much power is being used.  This can be very useful even if you aren’t building a solar system.  Everything plugged in at your house will consume power even if it’s not turned on, using this device to check things like TVs, amplifiers or computers can be very enlightening why enviro-crazy people unplug stuff when they aren’t home…it’s because they’re not actually crazy at all.

Step #1a: Separate AC and DC Electronics for Inverter Sizing

You likely noticed I made no mention of AC, DC or any wisdom from the last post, so I’ll toss that in here.  In my list above I have both AC (coffee grinder, phone charger) and DC (lights, water pump) appliances.  Since I made sure to buy car cigarette lighter power supplies for anything I could, I have very little use for an inverter, but I can still go through the simple exercise here.  Anywhere possible it’s best to purchase a DC power adaptor as inverters are somewhere around 85% efficient; powering a 90 Watt laptop would take 105 Watts using an inverter.  Not the end of the world, but it’s still a waste.  And also worth noting is that waste will always be dissipated as heat, so those 15 Watts will contribute to raising the temperature in your RV and if pushed too far potentially harming electrical components.

Inverters are rated in terms of the maximum number of AC Watts they can output.  Luckily AC and DC Watts are the same, save for the 85% efficiency problem, but they both represent the same amount of “work”.  So all you need to do is calculate the maximum total amount of AC watts you would ever use and buy a slightly larger inverter.  Expert tip, any inverter they sell at an auto parts shop or Walmart is marked up ridiculously, I bought a piece of junk 300 Watt one from China for $30, it works perfectly and I have capacity to spare.  Dealextreme.com baby, you will learn to love it, fast.

Step #2: Solar Panel Sizing

Now that we have our total daily power consumption number, we need to be able to supply it.  Sticking with my example spreadsheet, I came up with 140 amp hours a day of consumption (total watt hours of 1700 divided by 12 volts).

Peak sunlight hour map

The first and most important variable is going to be where your system is geographically.  In the middle of winter at the North Pole, there’s no sun whatsoever, but there’s still a fair bit in the Arizona desert.  Take a look at this map or these worksheets to see where you are locating your solar system and determine the total number of peak hours of sun you’ll get a day.  Because I’m aiming to be in the southern US during the winter, I took the rough guestimate that I’ll be getting 5 hours of peak sunlight a day.  Using this “peak sunlight” number makes life much easier math-wise, the sun might be up for 11 hours, but that equates to maybe 5 hours direct sunlight.  So 1700 watt hours divided by 5 hours equals 340 watts, the bare minimum of panel output needed.

From there we need to take into account cloudy days.  That map already does factor them in if you know your exact location, but since I’ll be moving around, I’ll just come up with a rough worst case that it’s cloudy for 3 days in a row, and that clouds cut solar input by 50%.  This isn’t an exact science, and depends on your battery bank size how much you can tap into them during stretches of low light, and how much you’re willing to conserve, but essentially because round numbers are easy I knew I’d be needing around 400 watts of panel.

Step #3: Battery Bank Sizing

In a solar off-grid system the batteries wind up being just as crucial to providing energy as the panels themselves.  When the sun goes down, you need to be able to draw on all the energy accumulated during the day to run your electronics.  A battery bank that is too small will pose two problems.  The first is that it will shorten the lifespan of batteries.  Avoid draining your batteries below 50% of their overall capacity.  If you have 200 amp hours of capacity, and always drain your budgeted 140 amp hours, this continual depletion of 75% of the available energy will cause the batteries to degrade, not store as much charge and need to be replaced much sooner.  The second problem with a battery bank too small is not harvesting all the available energy.  If it’s nice and sunny and you always fully recharge the batteries by 2 PM every day, from then until sunset you’re not getting any more power, it’s just waste.

A deep-cycle battery for storing solar energy

Conversely, there are two downsides to a battery bank that is too large.  The first problem is if you are unable to fully recharge the battery bank.  If you wind up depleting the batteries to 25%, then can only recharge them to 85% and repeat that cycle for many days in a row, it too will alter the battery chemistry, lessen the amount of charge they hold and shorten their lifespan.  Batteries like being fully charged, keep them happy.  The second problem with a battery bank too large is wasted cost and weight.  This will be covered in depth in the Choosing Batteries post, but 100 amp hours of capacity is going to weight about 60 lbs and cost $150.  If you have 800 amp hours of capacity (which is about twice as much as you need) then you essentially wasted $600 and are carrying an extra 240 lbs.

Ideally you want to find the happy place between too much and not enough capacity.  Make sure you won’t deplete below 50% capacity at night, make sure you can recharge to 100% during the day and you’re set.  Surprisingly for a 12 volt system the math basically works out that you want the same number of amp hours of battery capacity as you have solar watts, in my case roughly 400.  One of the reasons for this is a battery’s ideal charge rate is 10% of its total capacity, so 40 amps times 12 volts equals 480 watts, which is pretty close to what I’ll be generating from the panels.
