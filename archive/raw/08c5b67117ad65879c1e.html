The Ocean

Blimpy On September - 30 - 2010

The Pacific that is, not the excellent Led Zeppelin song.  Well, it could be any ocean for all I care.  Growing up a prairie boy, I’ve probably only seen the truly open water 20 times in my life, and it seems each time gets more memorable as my comprehension expands.  In fact, I happened to take two videos the last time I saw the ocean on the Aran Islands off the coast of Ireland a year and a half ago.  Call it part of the Blimpy Backstory, they definitely capture my elation and awe, well before I fancied myself a showman.  Aran Islands 1.  Aran Islands 2.

Sweet Jesus

Sure, Vancouver and Seattle are on the coast, but they’re harbours on sounds of inlets.  There aren’t even waves, that doesn’t count.  No, I’m talking about looking straight out past 15 foot breakers and knowing there’s nothing for thousands of miles.  To me this only accentuates the already striking aesthetic beauty.  Why it elicits this base emotional response in us as humans is beyond the scope of this post, I think.

In the Rocky Mountains, you literally can’t see the forest through the trees, that same sense is lacking even though the vastness of Mother Nature is comparable.  Comparable, but still.  The ocean is anything but still.  The constant churning, the chaos of each tumbling white cap, not only does its enormity convey space, but its continual action conveys time.

Come back in 50 years, it will be totally different

Fifty years ago these shores were a vast lake country, a hundred years ago a desert.  There is a tsunami warning system all the way along the coast so we know to take higher ground the next time She changes her mind.  Washington won’t be falling into the Pacific Ocean like much of California when the big one hits, she’ll just be taking quite the bath, to the point it’s even an issue in the November elections.  And who knew there was a Juan De Fuca tectonic plate?  Not I, but speaking of tectonic plates, did you know there is a fault in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean where magma bubbles to the ocean floor and is causing an ever growing mound similar to how the Hawaiian Islands were formed?  Except they already know this one is unstable and will eventually sheer, generating a tidal wave 10 times larger than can possibly be generated by an earthquake.

As I stand on these Washington shores for the first time, my bare feet feel the grains of sand are finer than any beach I’ve encountered before.  Why?  I soon learn it’s because most of the deposits are leftovers of what’s washed out to sea by the Columbia River, the 2nd largest on the continent behind the Mississippi.  It’s silt, pulverized shards of those same Rocky Mountains I struggled through myself months ago.  Silt, and no doubt a sprinkling of ground up abalone shells from Polynesia.

Madness when you think about it, beauty when you look at it

How did this bizarre mixture of yesterday’s coloured and shaped concrete matter come to fruition as today’s gray, entropy-ravished sand particles?  The Columbia and all its tributaries are propelled towards the ocean because the Earth is massive enough for gravity to force water downhill no matter what, taking whatever it will in its path over countless millennia.  Likewise, the gravity of sister moon is pulling the entire ocean back and forth continuously, wave by wave.  Then for good measure the heat from the sun strikes the Earth, water and cloud to create temperature differences that lead to ocean currents and winds that traverse the entire globe.  Sand, brought to you by invisible forces from outer space.

Woah.

Mother Nature, 1.  Us, 0.
