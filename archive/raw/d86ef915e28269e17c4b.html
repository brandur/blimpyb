Bourbon Ginger Ale Bacon

Blimpy On August - 11 - 2010

The real point of this recipe isn’t the ingredients, or how they were cooked.  It’s more just in the spirit of screwing around, which to me is a key ingredient of good cooking, experimentation.  I was having fun throwing whatever I could into a fire pit, so I ran with it.  After the success of the chuck roast, I thought I’d take a funny twist on everybody’s favourite salty smoked pork, iiiiiiiit’s bacon!

The most important ingredient in life

Ingredients

  • 1 lb pork belly (I went with brined, if not you’ll have to add salt)
  • 1 tsp garlic powder
  • 2 tsp mustard powder
  • 10 whole white peppercorns
  • 1 tsp crushed black pepper
  • 2 sprigs fresh rosemary
  • 1 tbsp bourbon molasses mustard (half that amount of molasses or brown sugar would work)
  • ½ oz bourbon
  • 2 oz ginger ale

Preparation

Delicious absorption

Pork bellies are the uncooked version of the cut you would immediately recognize as bacon.  It’s literally the belly of a pig, skin on one side, super fatty layers interspersed with meat on the other.  As seems to be becoming a theme of these recipes, it’s also dirt cheap.  I had always seen it as a lose packaged cut next to the hocks and other random pork bits, but at the Real Canadian Wholesale whose parking lot I was living in they had it pre-salted in a sealed bag filled with brine.  It didn’t affect the pork flavour at all, it just makes it salty and cured, so for something like bacon it’s ideal.

Once you’ve bought the pork, take it out of the package, wash it, pat it down with paper towel and trim off any bits you don’t want in the final product.  In a small dish, sprinkle the dry ingredients over all sides of the pork to form a thing coating.  The same can then be done with the rosemary and molasses mustard, just making sure it’s evenly distributed.  Lastly pour the bourbon and ginger ale over top, cover and let sit for anywhere from 6 to 24 hours in the fridge.  Rotate the pork in the marinade at intervals.  The bourbon will slightly break down the meat and add more flavour, as will the acidity of the ginger ale as it adds sweetness.

Cooking

A delicate balancing act

This is the simplest part of the recipe, it just takes time.  I chose to get an open campfire going just like the chuck roast, but this time used a perforated tin foil tray.  They sell these premade at Walmart called a Grill Buddy, they’re a worthwhile investment.  I placed the meat above the fire pit, on the opposite side of the fire itself and downwind.  A smoker would obviously also work and allow better temperature regulation, but like I said, I’m just screwing around.

I used the same fire to cook up ribs and vegetables, so had plenty of time to leave the bacon up top in the smoke both before and after the cook time of the other stuff.  I left it up there about 4.5 hours total, which proved to either be slightly too long, or at slightly too high a temperature, but I still achieved the desired effect.  The bacon was both cooked and smoked and slightly tough, but it’s not really the type of stuff you would have as a side with eggs and toast, too rich and too fatty.  Instead it ended up being more like a pancetta, something I could cut one slice of, dice and fry up with a pasta sauce or risotto to give it an incredibly rich and tasty smoked pork flavour.

Great result, along with the bonus peach I smoked for fun

One word of warning (and why it’s probably best to use the smoked pork in this way as an accompaniment) is that the process of smoking, especially the way I did it with normal firewood, basically just replaces a lot of the water and other molecules in the meat with cancer.  Delicious cancer nonetheless, but whatever gets absorbed on the outside of the meat over the course of those many hours can’t possibly be any good for you.  I would recommend scraping any residue off the outside of the meat before using, but used as a mega flavour enhancer as opposed to a standalone piece of pork, this dish proved its weight in gold.
