Canon Extension Tube

Blimpy On January - 19 - 2011

I’m going to let you in on a little secret about the newest toy I got for my Canon T2i camera, the macro extension tube: There’s almost no reason whatsoever to buy the actual Canon Extension Tube versus a no name brand like Kenko or Opteka! For less than the price of one Canon Extension Tube, you get a set of three different lengths that can be combined together while still maintaining the electrical connections for aperture and autofocus control, the only downside being the use of plastic in the construction.  Even better, unless you’re a professional, you can easily delay the need to get a dedicated macro lens when a set of macro extension tubes can give you about half of the same functionality at one tenth the price.

For the uninitiated, a macro extension tube is a hollow piece of metal and/or plastic that you attach to your SLR in between the lens and camera body.  This changes the focal length of the lens, so a 50mm lens with a 25 extension tube will act like a 75mm lens in terms of magnification, but with two major differences.  The first is that it will no longer be able to focus to infinity and depending on the tube/lens focal length combo may only be able to focus feet or inches away.  Secondly, your minimum focusing distance will also decrease.

On my Sigma 30 lens where I usually need to be a foot away from something, I can lessen this to a matter of inches with a 12 extension tube.  While this isn’t useful for birds or bugs, for plants, found objects and textures, the results can be outstanding, especially if you’re just starting out and wanting to play around.  I’d almost want to call it “hackro” to coin a new term, but as soon as you start hitting magnification factors higher than 1:1, you’ve achieved the textbook definition of macro.

I purchased a set of 13mm, 21mm and 31mm Opteka extension tubes for $80 and haven’t looked back since.  They also come rebranded as CowboyStudio, but are the exact same as Opteka.  I even went to several of the local camera stores in San Diego which sold the Kenko extension tubes and found them to be almost identical, but costing over twice the price.  And worse still are the Canon Extension Tubes which would run at over $200 for just a 12mm and a 25mm.

I’ve started a gallery page of macro sample shots achieved with the extension tubes that I’ll be expanding on over time, but even the classic example of the penny turns out pretty well using the 18-55 kit lens.  Sure, the sharpness and contrast aren’t out of this world due to the lens (especially noticeable on the 2nd and 3rd oranges below) but it’s an incredible amount of fun and impressive to me nonetheless as all of a sudden I have a camera that’s also a magnifying glass.

The only other drawback to the extension tube approach is when you have to get that close to the subject, the depth of field is incredibly short.  This can still be compensated for with aperture, but only so much.  You can sometimes use this to your advantage to achieve interesting effects like the hibiscus blossom below, and in a future post I’ll outline another trick of depth of field blending using Photoshop and a tripod taking multiple pictures.

Just to summarize why I feel a Canon Extension Tube (or ever better the generic version from Opteka) is a great investment and addition to any of the lenses in your bag, here’s a quick bullet list of the pros and cons.

Benefits

  • increased magnification
  • lower minimum focusing distance (MFD)
  • maintain electrical connection for aperture and autofocus control
  • works with EF-S lenses

Drawbacks

  • heavy lenses (i.e. glass telephoto over 300mm) might stress the camera body and tubes
  • the longer the lens focal length, the less the magnification increase
  • depth of field is limited (but this is often part of macro photography)
