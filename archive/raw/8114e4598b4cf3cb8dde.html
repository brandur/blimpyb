The Solar Series: Power – AC & DC

Blimpy On August - 24 - 2010

I actually wrote the System Sizing post before this one, then realized due to the curse of the engineer I’d taken for granted a lot of the background information about electricity.  If you understand all of this already you can skip to the next post, but I’m going to try in plain English to give a quick and dirty explanation of how electrical power works in its different forms.  Wikipedia can also help you out on power, AC, DC and inverters.

Power

How a light bulb works

Why does a light bulb get bright and hot when you flip the switch?  The basic principle of electricity is that current flows from a higher voltage to a lower voltage, in this case through the tungsten filament in the bulb.  The filament resists this flow like rapids in a river, so the electricity that freely flows through the copper wires in the wall leading up to the socket is all of a sudden confronted with a different filament material trying to stop it.  In essence the electric field in the wire smashes into all the molecules in the filament and transfers electric energy into kinetic energy (causing a drop in voltage), which makes the tungsten vibrate at an atomic level.  This vibration causes the electrons within the tungsten to jump to higher energy level, but as they are unstable in this state they will fall back down to their normal level and in doing so release light and heat.  This cycle repeats continuously like a hot air popcorn popper, the electrons get pushed to the top by electricity, then fall back down of their own accord and give off energy only to be pushed back up again.

This continual cycle can be put in a more simplified term of just “doing work”.  Making light, making heat, moving something, doing work.  The amount of work done in one second is called a Watt.  In the light bulb example which is typically 100 Watts, this equates to approximately 1020 electrons jumping state per second.

Alternating Current (AC)

The difference between AC and DC

The electrical outlets in your house powering the light bulb run 110 Volt alternating current at 60 Hertz (i.e. the frequency, 60 times per second) from the North American power grid.  This means one prong of your socket varies in a sine wave from +110 to -110 and back to +120, 60 times per second, with this wire referred to as “hot”.  The other prong is called “ground” and stays at 0 Volts.  Current flows from the higher potential (110 volts) to ground and in doing so performs work.  Eventually as the voltage of the hot wire oscillates to be below zero, current will flow in the reverse direction from the now higher potential 0 Volts at ground to the -110 hot.  The same process of smashing into the resistive tungsten I described in the Power section still occurs, it’s just swooshing back and forth in both directions 60 times a second.

The main reason the power in our homes is AC is because it comes from spinning generator turbines at a power plant somewhere.  Water falls down a dam or coal heats steam which spins a bunch of giant magnets at 60 times per second, so they just send that down the lines.  For more super fun tangent times, the whole Edison versus Tesla battle in the early 20th Century is a very interesting one.  I prefer Tesla due to his work on providing free power to the world by energizing the ionosphere, or using the same technology for some sort of death ray that was confiscated by the US government upon his death in the 1940′s with still no details released to this day.  Electricity is more sordid than you thought, no?

Direct Current (DC)

Anything that’s powered by a battery (such as Bessie the RV) instead runs on DC power, which is much simpler to understand.  The “hot” terminal is always 12 Volts, the ground always zero volts, there’s no switching, everything flows one way down hill.  Pretty simple.  Interestingly enough, in any home electronic appliance (TV, stereo, computer) the very first thing on the other end of the wall AC cord is an AC to DC converter, a stable DC voltage is needed to run anything of precision.  Unfortunately, if you want to take your home stereo into your RV, there’s really no way to take it apart and wire it straight into the RV’s 12 volt DC, you need one more piece…

Inverter

AC vs DC, Inverter vs Cigarette Lighter

The missing link is something called an inverter.  It is a piece of electronic equipment able to take 12 volt DC power and convert it to 110 volt AC power.  Now anything that plugs into the wall in your home can plug into your off-grid solar system.  The sizing of an inverter will be covered in the next post, but it’s very important to realize (and the entire point of this post) that within an off-grid system there can and likely will be both AC and DC electronics to be run.  While it’s pretty obvious which is which when you try and plug a two-prong AC cord into a cigarette lighter socket and get nowhere, being aware of this beforehand and what’s actually happening with all the electrons jumping all over the place will definitely help you build a better off-grid system.

Solar

Since this is The Solar Series, I thought I would finish by tying up the loose end of how the sun and solar panels fit in this cycle.  Due to something called the photoelectric effect (discovered by Einstein no less) when sun shines on certain types of metal, that metal will produce an electrical voltage.  It’s actually the exact reverse principle of a light bulb.  The sun imparts kinetic energy on the electrons within a solar panel causing them to jump up in energy level, which again is unstable and they will eventually fall back to their normal state.  Rather than glowing or getting hot, due to the different material properties of a solar panel the energy level drop is released as electricity.

For a lot more background on how solar panels work in general that I won’t be getting into here, try this online Learning Center.
